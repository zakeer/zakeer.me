Story 1 — Driving MFE Adoption
Cross-team architecture change you drove yourself
micro-frontend adoption convince management large-scale architecture
Staff framing: "I noticed this before anyone asked me to."

This started from something I noticed on my own — nobody asked me to look into it. We had six teams all building different parts of the same product, but there was no shared agreement on how those pieces should work together. Every team had its own way of doing things. I kept seeing the same UI components built three different ways, and every time two teams needed to work together on something, it took far longer than it should.

I started writing down what this was actually costing us. How often releases slipped because one team was waiting on another. How many hours a week teams spent just figuring out why things didn\'t fit together. Once I had real numbers, I put together a document: here\'s what\'s happening, here are a few options, here\'s what I\'d recommend and why.

The technical work — setting up Module Federation — wasn't the hardest part once I\'d done the research. I looked at Angular\'s built-in lazy-loading and a couple of other approaches before settling on this one, because I needed to explain clearly why the others didn\'t work as well. The harder part was convincing six teams who were already stretched thin to take on something new.

What shifted things was making the current cost visible. When people can see a real number — this is how many hours a week we\'re losing right now — the conversation changes. It stops being about whether to change and starts being about how to do it without making things worse first.

We started with one team as a proper test. Documented what broke, fixed the setup, then expanded. By the end, teams were shipping on their own schedule. Code overlap dropped about 20%, and the shared component library we built got used by teams outside our area too. That\'s still running in production today — and I don\'t own it anymore. Which is exactly what you want.

Story 2 — API Contract Standards
Influence without authority / alignment across teams
influence without authority cross-team alignment process change
Staff framing: "This was a cross-team problem, not just mine."

We had three teams at Netskope — design, backend, and frontend — all working from different assumptions about what the data between them would look like. Not because anyone was being careless. Nobody had said it was anyone\'s job to define this. It kept showing up as bugs: a field name didn\'t match, something one team expected to always have a value would come back empty, and both sides would spend half a day figuring out who was wrong.

I wasn\'t the manager. Nobody asked me to fix it. But I could trace specific bugs back to this problem, and the pattern was clear enough. I wrote up a simple standard — how we\'d define what data looks like between systems, how we\'d handle changes, how we\'d flag when something was going to break.

Then I talked to each team separately before any group meeting. I\'d learned this from earlier mistakes — if you walk into a room with a proposal and three teams haven\'t seen it, you\'re not going to get alignment, you\'re just going to have a messy debate. Getting feedback one-on-one means that by the time you\'re all in the same room, you\'re actually solving the remaining real issues, not fighting about whether the idea is good.

The joint meeting ended up being straightforward. The standard became how we do new features. The recurring integration bugs that showed up every cycle basically stopped. Teams started caring about keeping their interfaces clean, instead of just arguing about who broke what.

Story 3 — Production Incident
Critical failure, rollback, and what changed after
production incident critical failure post-mortem
Staff framing: "The harder part wasn\'t the technical work — it was what came after."

We had a production problem where an update I pushed to a shared component broke dashboards across several products. Not subtle — it was immediate, visible, and multiple teams were affected right away.

First thing I did was get the right people on a call quickly. Not everyone who was upset, just whoever could actually do something about it. Too many people in an incident slows things down — you spend time sorting out who\'s doing what instead of just doing it. I kept the working group small and sent regular status updates separately to everyone else. We rolled back within an hour.

Rolling back is the easy part. What I cared more about was making sure the same thing couldn\'t happen again. We ran a review — and I mean genuinely focused on the system, not on finding someone to blame. I've been in reviews where they say it\'s blameless but you can feel it isn\'t, so people defend themselves instead of being honest. I set the tone early: the process failed, we\'re here to understand why.

The real finding was that we had no discipline around how changes to shared components got shipped. Teams were always using the latest version, which is fine until you need to reverse something fast and suddenly you can\'t. We fixed it: clear version numbers, a definition of what counts as a breaking change, and a process for handling those. Two months later we had another update cycle — completely uneventful. That\'s the real test.

Story 4 — Code Review Improvement
Raising the quality floor / multiplying team impact
team quality code review process multiplying impact
Staff framing: "I built the case, got alignment, and made it the default — not a one-time fix."

When I joined Netskope, code reviews were happening but inconsistently. One senior engineer would flag a performance problem; another would let the same pattern through without a comment. New engineers had no reliable signal for what good looked like — it depended entirely on who reviewed their code that week.

I spent two weeks watching before I suggested anything. What was being flagged, what wasn\'t, where bugs we fixed later had already been visible during review and nobody caught them. Two things stood out: there was no shared definition of what a review was actually checking, and there was no expected timeframe, so code sat for two or three days and blocked people.

I introduced review checklists covering the things we\'d agreed mattered — performance, security, accessibility, test coverage. Then automated checks for everything a computer can reliably catch, so human review time stopped going to formatting and basic errors. And a 48-hour expectation on first review. That last one sounds small but it changed things noticeably. Now there was accountability in both directions, not just the person waiting.

New engineer onboarding went from four weeks to two, because there was a real standard to aim for instead of guessing what different people wanted. Bug-fix cycle time dropped about 30%. The same problem existed at SAP — large HR datasets were killing scroll performance. I introduced virtualized lists, render time dropped 25%, and that became the standard across the department. Both cases were the same kind of fix: address the problem at the system level and make sure it becomes the default.

Story 5 — AI in Production (SAP)
Responsible AI adoption, eval harness, enterprise HR at scale
AI in production eval harness enterprise scale
Staff framing: "The integration wasn\'t the hard part — the testing problem was."

I have two AI stories. One is about AI in the product itself, and one is about AI in how we develop. Let me do the product one first.

At SAP we had over 8,000 enterprise HR clients, and their teams were doing a lot of manual work that could be automated — routing requests, flagging unusual patterns, matching candidates to jobs. I proposed building a layer on top of GPT and a natural language processing library to handle this automatically.

The technical connection itself wasn\'t the hard part. What I actually spent most of my time on was the testing problem. When you\'re touching HR at enterprise scale, you\'re dealing with real employee data — pay, performance reviews, hiring decisions. You can\'t ship something where your confidence level is "it usually works." Before anything went to live, I built a testing setup: an automated scoring system that ran the AI\'s output against a labeled test set, wired into our build process so every model update got scored before it could deploy. If accuracy dropped below a threshold, the build failed.

That testing setup outlived the single feature I built it for — the team used the same approach for later AI work. Manual effort for clients who adopted the system dropped around 25%. What I'm most satisfied with is that we could show clients exactly how we measured accuracy, what our targets were, and how we\'d know if it got worse. That\'s a very different conversation from just saying "trust us, we tested it."

Story 5B — Spec-Driven Development Pipeline
AI in the development process itself — your strongest differentiator
Most unique story AI in dev process Figma → tickets Akash\'s area
Staff framing: "I noticed the translation work and decided it shouldn\'t be done by hand."

The other AI story — the one I find more interesting, honestly — is about the development process itself, not the product.

The problem I was trying to fix: going from a design to something engineers could actually build was slow and full of gaps. You\'d have a Figma design, and then someone had to write a requirements document, then a technical doc, then create work tickets, then write test cases — one after another, by hand, and every step was a chance for something to drift or just get skipped. Engineers were spending a lot of time on translation work that felt like it should be automatable.

I built a pipeline where an AI system starts from a walkthrough of the design and generates the requirements document, the technical spec, the work tickets, and the test cases automatically. Engineers review and correct instead of writing from scratch. That\'s actually a different kind of work — you\'re checking what\'s there rather than deciding what to include. You catch different things, and you catch more of them.

Post-release UI bugs dropped 25% and we went from monthly releases to weekly. The bug reduction surprised me more than the frequency change. My best guess is that generating test cases directly from the spec catches edge cases that a person writing tests from memory would miss — but I can\'t claim I proved that, it\'s just what the data suggested.

The thing that makes this interesting at Agoda\'s scale — a thousand experiments running at once — is that spec and ticket volume is probably ten times what we had. That translation work doesn\'t get smaller as you scale. It compounds.

Story 6 — Handling Pushback from Management
Defending a recommendation / data-driven persuasion
management pushback defend recommendation data-driven case
Staff framing: "I built the case properly instead of moving on conviction alone."

When I was proposing the MFE shift, management\'s concern was added complexity and the risk of disrupting teams already behind on their work. And honestly, it was a fair concern. Splitting an app into separate deployable pieces does add real complexity up front.

I didn\'t try to argue them out of it. The concern was correct. What I tried to show instead was the cost of where we already were. I put together actual numbers: how many hours a week were going into cross-team coordination right now, how often releases were slipping because of dependencies between teams. Not guesses about future problems — current, measurable cost that was already happening.

When there\'s a real number attached to a problem, the conversation shifts. It stops being about whether to change and starts being about how to manage the transition without making things worse first. I suggested starting with one team as a real test, with a clear decision point before we\'d expand. That gave management a way out if it wasn\'t working, and gave me a chance to prove it in practice before asking for a bigger commitment.

The pilot worked, and we expanded. Looking back — the fact that they pushed back hard actually helped. It meant I had to build the case properly instead of just moving on gut feeling, and the adoption held up better because of it.

Story 7 — Mentoring & Knowledge Transfer
Scaling your impact through others
mentoring knowledge sharing team growth
Staff framing: "I look for when they fix something without flagging it — that\'s when the knowledge is actually there."

I've tried a lot of different approaches to knowledge sharing, and what I keep running into is that presentations don\'t really stick. You do a session, people get it in the room, and three weeks later nothing has changed in how they work.

What worked better at Netskope was building things in front of people with the thinking visible. I\'d take something real from our codebase — like migrating a module from one build tool to another — work through it live, make decisions out loud, explain what I was weighing and why I went one way over another, then hand it off for someone else to continue from there. The doing stays with people. The watching doesn\'t.

I also started a session every two weeks where anyone could bring a problem or something they\'d learned — no agenda, no required slides. That one surfaced things I wouldn\'t have found any other way. Engineers earlier in their careers often know exactly where the daily pain is before senior people do, because they\'re the ones actually running into it every day.

The signal I look for: when someone catches a problem in a code review and doesn\'t even flag it as anything special — they just fixed it. That means the knowledge is actually there, not just the habit of waiting for someone else to notice it.

Design a Hotel Search Listing Page from Scratch
THE canonical Agoda question · High probability of repeat
HIGH PROBREPEAT

Step 1 — State constraints first

"Before I start drawing anything, let me state what I'm optimizing for. The page has two states: before a search (empty or showing popular destinations), and after a search (live results with filters and prices). Prices change frequently. Multiple teams will own different parts. Some city-level pages like /hotels/bangkok need to be indexed by Google."

Step 2 — Rendering strategy

I\'d recommend Next.js with a hybrid approach. City landing pages (/hotels/bangkok) use SSR — they load fast, work without JavaScript, and Google can index them. For live search results that depend on what the user typed, those render client-side after the initial page. I\'d use ISR for city pages so content updates without a full redeploy.

Step 3 — Repo and team structure

Monorepo. The search bar, hotel cards, filter panel, and pricing widget are separate packages. Teams own their package. Module Federation lets them deploy independently. The host application defines where each piece goes — it doesn\'t own the pieces themselves.

Step 4 — State management

  • Filter state → URL params. /hotels/bangkok?checkin=2026-07-01&guests=2. Shareable, bookmarkable, back button works. No library needed.
  • Hotel list data → React Query. Handles caching, loading states, retries. I don\'t need Redux for server data.
  • Session state (selected hotel, comparison) → Zustand. Simple, no boilerplate.

Step 5 — API layer

I\'d introduce a BFF here. The hotel listing page needs data from search, pricing, inventory, and image services — four different backends. Without a BFF, the frontend makes four calls and assembles the response itself. The BFF combines them and sends exactly what the frontend needs. It also lives close to the backend services, so it\'s faster. For Agoda this is likely in Kotlin or .NET.

Step 6 — Price freshness

Two-step: load the hotel list quickly with cached prices, then fetch live prices in the background and update just those fields. For users actively browsing, poll every 45 seconds or use a WebSocket connection. The list never goes blank while prices refresh.

Metrics I\'d define from day one

  • First Contentful Paint under 1.5 seconds
  • Search results visible in under 2 seconds, P95
  • Booking conversion rate — the north star. Everything supports this.

Testing

Contract tests between the BFF and each backend service — if pricing changes what it returns, we catch it before it reaches the frontend. Visual regression tests for hotel cards. E2E tests for the full search → select → book path.

Migration from monolith

No big rewrite. Pick this page — high value, high traffic — rebuild it in the new architecture, run both versions in parallel with a traffic split, cut over when stable. The monolith keeps running until migration is done page by page.

  • Don\'t say "maybe" for any architectural decision — say "I'm recommending X because Y."
  • Mention you evaluated Module Federation against alternatives — that signals Staff-level thinking.
  • Bring up BFF proactively — Agoda uses it. Score points before being asked.
  • Bring up metrics before being asked — FCP <1.5s, search-to-results P95, booking conversion as north star.
  • If you blank: "Let me anchor back to the core constraint — the decision here is..."

Before Culture Round

  • Every story starts with a scope sentence: "This was a cross-team problem, not just mine..."
  • At least one story should have: "I noticed this before anyone asked me to look at it."
  • Tell one story in experiment frame: hypothesis → what we measured → what changed → what we'd do differently.
  • Replace "I tried to ensure" with "I built the case, got teams aligned, and the result was..."
  • If AI comes up: lead with Spec-Driven Development pipeline first. Akash's team works on agent AI frontends.

Before Platform Round

  • Replace "maybe we could" with "I\'d recommend" for every technical suggestion.
  • Volunteer security and testing angles before being asked — it signals Staff-level thinking.
  • Have a crisp BFF explanation ready — 2 sentences maximum.
  • If AI questions arise: LLM-as-Judge eval harness + CI integration is your technical depth signal.
